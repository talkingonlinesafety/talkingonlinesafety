About the workshop

Online safety, examined at its edges

Online safety research and practice have advanced considerably, yet critical questions remain at the edges. Which communities are still not designed for when safety tools are built? And as AI becomes central to online safety, how do we reckon with its dual role as both a source of new harms and a proposed remedy?

Responding to the OzCHI 2026 theme “On the Edge”, this workshop brings together researchers, practitioners, policymakers, and community advocates across disciplines to examine online safety at its limits. Building on our OzCHI 2025 workshop, we will centre case studies and scenarios of exclusion, focusing on communities that safety tools were rarely built around: women and gender-diverse people, culturally and linguistically diverse communities, people with disability, children, older populations, and more.

Together we will

  • Identify communities needing greater attention in online safety.
  • Interrogate AI safety tools with the same scrutiny we apply to the harms they address.
  • Develop interdisciplinary collaboration across academia, industry, policy, and community.
  • Build sustained networks and a shared research agenda.

Who should join

Researchers, designers, policymakers, industry and community advocates working on online safety, from HCI, Social Computing, Design, Policy, Cybersecurity, Ethics, the Social Sciences and beyond. Emerging and established researchers, new and returning participants are all welcome.

What you'll do

Work in interdisciplinary groups on real-world case studies, develop research proposals, and engage with experts from academia, policy, community advocacy, and industry.

Topics of interest

Four lenses on inclusive online safety

Topics include, but are not limited to, the areas below. Select an area to see its topics.

Designing for inclusion, safety, and wellbeing
  • User-centred and participatory approaches to understanding and responding to online abuse
  • Safety by design principles and their practical implementation across platforms
  • Participatory and co-design methods for developing safety interventions with affected communities
  • Designing for resilience and digital literacy in online safety
  • Prevention-focused approaches to online safety
  • Evaluation methods for assessing safety intervention effectiveness
  • Psychological impacts of online harm and trauma-informed design
  • Cultural and contextual considerations in online safety design, particularly for communities that need greater attention
Policy, governance, and industry
  • Policy frameworks and regulatory approaches to online safety
  • Inclusion of equity-focused safety standards and obligations for protecting communities that need greater attention
  • Industry perspectives on implementing and scaling safety features
  • Cross-platform safety considerations and interoperability
  • Collaborative models involving academia, industry, policymakers, and communities
AI, technology, and ethics
  • Emerging online safety challenges from generative AI and synthetic media
  • Use of AI for enhancing online safety and harm detection
  • The dual role of AI as both a source of harm and a proposed safeguard
  • Transparency, explainability, and contestability in safety-related AI systems
  • Privacy-preserving safety interventions
  • Bias and the uneven effects of AI safety tools across different communities
  • Ethical considerations in developing safety technologies, especially for marginalised or underserved users
  • Evaluating the impact and unintended consequences of AI-driven safety tools
Social, behavioural, and cultural perspectives
  • Social and behavioural dynamics of online abuse (perpetrator, bystander, and survivor perspectives)
  • Cross-cultural differences in perceptions of safety, harm, and acceptable behaviour online
  • Structural, cultural, and socioeconomic factors influencing online safety for underrepresented communities
  • Psychological impacts of sustained exposure to online harm and strategies for resilience
  • Online safety education, awareness, and digital literacy interventions from a behavioural perspective
  • Collective action, social movements, and advocacy around online safety and digital rights

We particularly encourage submissions describing interdisciplinary work involving multiple stakeholders across academia, industry, policy, and community advocacy, and work that engages directly with the communities most affected by online harm.

Take part

How to take part

Submit a short Expression of Interest. Along with your name, affiliation, research or professional area, a brief bio, and any access requirements, the form asks three short questions:

  1. Describe a recent event, case study, or news article involving an online harm and a platform or tool implicated in it (or one whose design raises safety concerns for particular groups), and why it matters.
  2. Reflect on it through the lens of your own field.
  3. Tell us what you hope to gain from the workshop.

You can draw on your own work where relevant. Prior research on the case is welcome but not required.
